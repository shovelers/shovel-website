How we think · 02

Intent has no receipt.

Smriti · Head of Product & Design @Shovel

September 2026 · an 8-minute read

Consumer AI is still at the beginning. It may become one of the largest global technology categories. It is also one of the hardest to build.

The challenge is deeper than model intelligence or task automation. Consumer products ultimately shape a human experience. Every screen, word, voice, logo and interaction contributes to an emotion: trust, satisfaction, status, relief, confidence or joy.

01A playbook that does not exist yet

Traditional consumer software has spent decades refining this experience. Teams optimise screens pixel by pixel. They know where a user lands, what draws attention, when an offer feels exciting and which steps increase conversion. The templates are mature. Following them will not create an exceptional product, but it can take you a long way.

That playbook works for a reason that is easy to miss. The outcome it optimises is visible inside the same system that produces it. A purchase is a row in a table. A drop-off is a missing row. Every experiment can be settled by counting, because the thing being counted is the thing that mattered.

Consumer AI does not yet have an equivalent playbook. It has borrowed the vocabulary — sessions, retention, task success — but the outcome it should be optimising is not in the table.

02Intent has low verifiability

Consumer behaviour is rarely linear. Consider someone buying a product online. They search, compare, leave, return, change filters, share an item, add it to a wishlist and sometimes never come back. We can observe the funnel, but understanding the real outcome is much harder.

Did they find the right product?Did they lose interest?Did the price feel wrong?Did they decide to buy it elsewhere?Or were they never ready to purchase?

This creates a fundamental problem: consumer intent has low verifiability.

It helps to lay outcomes on a line. At one end sit the things a system can verify on its own: the order arrived, the payment cleared, the cab came, the answer took two seconds. In the middle sit things that can be verified with effort: it was the cheapest option that day, the substitute was equivalent, the message reached the right person. At the far end sit the things that can only be inferred: it was the right thing to buy, it served the household, they would choose it again. Precision, latency and reliability live at the verifiable end. The experience lives at the other.

When the correct outcome cannot be observed clearly, it becomes difficult to evaluate the system, improve its recommendations or train it to perform better. Teams do what teams have always done when the real label is missing: they substitute one they can see. Clicks stand in for interest. Re-orders stand in for satisfaction. A thumbs-up stands in for a good decision. The proxy then becomes the target, and the measure stops measuring.1 Consumer software spent a decade learning this lesson under the name of engagement. Consumer AI will be offered the same bargain, with a more persuasive product on the other side of it.

Even the vendors describe what they hold as approximate. OpenAI’s own documentation says memory suits high-level preferences and details rather than exact records, and that chat history does not retain every detail.2 That is an honest description of a system built for conversation. It is not a description of a system that knows whether it served you.

03What a screen can say that a conversation cannot

Agentic consumer products make this challenge even more demanding.

Chat is a limited interface for many consumer decisions. A screen can communicate dozens of options, comparisons and visual cues at once. A conversation reveals information sequentially. Voice is more natural, but it introduces interruption, tone, timing, ambiguity and context that may never be stated explicitly.

Put simply, a screen is parallel and a conversation is serial. Thirty products on a grid cost the eye a second; thirty products in a chat cost thirty turns. Voice is serial and lossy on top of that — people begin speaking a median 151 milliseconds before the other party has finished,3 and much of what they mean is carried by tone and by what they leave out. Yet voice is the only interface that works while hands are busy, which is most of household life.

The consequence is not that chat and voice are wrong for consumer decisions. It is that the interface can show less of the choice, so the intelligence behind it must carry more of the decision. An agent that cannot lay out thirty options has to already know which three are worth saying aloud. That knowledge does not come from the model. It comes from context.

04Automation is the first layer

Task automation is therefore only the first layer of consumer AI.

Anything that can be reduced to clicks will increasingly be automated. Searching, browsing, filling forms, placing orders, scheduling and tracking are becoming basic capabilities. Over the next decade, much of this interaction will disappear into the system.

It is worth saying how early even this layer is. On OSWorld 2.0, a set of 108 computer-use tasks with a median human completion time of 1.6 hours, the best model completes 20.6 per cent, and no model completes anything that takes a person longer than 163 minutes.4 On Mind2Web 2, Operator completes 10 per cent against a 54 per cent human baseline.5 Across the 214 realistic tasks of AssistantBench, no system scored above 26.6 These are professional workflows rather than household ones, and they measure intelligence rather than dependability. But the shape holds: the longer the task, the less often it finishes.

The failure is rarely access. When Swiggy opened its services to ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini in January 2026, a reporter’s test needed developer mode to begin, could not add items to the cart and could pay only on delivery.7 The door was open. The task still did not complete.

Automating a task is different from making a good decision.

05A task is not a decision

The harder problem is understanding what a person is trying to achieve, what matters within their household, what constraints apply and what a good outcome means for them. The system must then complete the action reliably and learn from whether the result was actually useful.

The arithmetic makes this unforgiving. A household runs on jobs that depend on each other. If the basket is wrong, the meal fails, and the evening fails with it. So the quality of a week is not the average of its tasks; it is closer to their product. A system that lands each job 80 per cent of the time lands a whole week of ten about one time in nine. At 90 per cent, one in three. At 95, three in five.8 Being good at most things is not a weaker version of this product. It is a different one.

Trust behaves differently from every other factor in that product. It multiplies like the rest, but it moves like a ratchet: it collapses in a single incident and rebuilds over months.910 A product that is right 95 per cent of the time and wrong once, expensively, does not sit at 95 per cent in the household’s mind.

Notice what a decision requires that a task does not. A task needs a connector and a credential. A decision needs to know that this household is vegetarian on Tuesdays, that the parents visit in March, that the budget tightened last quarter, that the one thing that must never be substituted is the child’s milk. None of that is a preference someone typed into a settings screen. It is structured household state, held with provenance and permission. Across today’s general assistants there is no evidence that any of it is collected as structure; what they hold is inferred from conversation, and their own documentation says as much.2 Not because they cannot — because a product built for everyone defers exactly the choices a product built for a house has to make.

06What the next generation will need

The next generation of consumer AI will require more than capable models and large connector libraries. It will need four things, and each is harder than the one before it.

Deep personal context — a record, not a memory. Facts about the household with a source, a confidence and a permission attached, so that what the system acts on can be inspected and corrected.

Precise evaluation — labels for the unobservable. Every consequential action broken into what was asked, why this option, what was done, and whether it served, so that the loop has something to learn from.

Dependable completion — recovery, confirmation and verification owned by the product rather than left to the model or the connector. The click layer is shared with everyone. Reliability is not.

A product experience that earns trust over long periods — a receipt for everything that mattered, in plain language, at the moment it happened.

The opportunity is enormous. The standards for getting it right will be equally high.

Intent has no receipt. The work of consumer AI is to give it one.
